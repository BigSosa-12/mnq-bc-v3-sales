What Is Futures Trading?

Futures are contracts that allow traders to speculate on the future price of an asset, such as a stock index, gold, or oil.

EX1.1 focuses on MNQ (Micro E-mini Nasdaq-100 futures), which tracks the Nasdaq-100 index.

Unlike buying a stock and hoping its value increases, futures traders can take positions in either direction.

  • LONG (BUY): you expect the price to rise.
  • SHORT (SELL): you expect the price to fall.

Example: if MNQ rises from 25,000 to 25,050, that is a 50-point upward move. One MNQ contract is worth $2 per index point, so a long position would gain $100 before fees. A short position would lose $100.

Futures are leveraged, so losses can accumulate quickly, and traders can lose more than their initial margin.

Understanding Candlesticks

Each candle represents price movement during a specific period.

  • Green candle: price closed higher than it opened.
  • Red candle: price closed lower than it opened.
  • Body: distance between the opening and closing prices.
  • Wicks: highest and lowest prices reached during that candle.

A long wick can suggest price rejection, but it does not guarantee a reversal.

A 5-minute chart shows one candle for every five minutes of trading.

03

Market Structure: Reading Price Direction

Price typically moves in waves rather than straight lines.

  • Bullish structure: higher highs (HH) and higher lows (HL). Buyers are generally maintaining upward pressure.
  • Bearish structure: lower highs (LH) and lower lows (LL). Sellers are generally maintaining downward pressure.
  • Range or consolidation: price moves sideways between relatively clear highs and lows.

Understanding structure helps you avoid buying directly into resistance or selling directly into support.

04

Key Trading Terms

Support

A price area where buying interest has previously appeared.

Resistance

A price area where selling interest has previously appeared.

Retest

When price returns to a previously broken level to test whether that level will hold.

VWAP

Volume-Weighted Average Price: the average traded price weighted by volume over a defined period, commonly the session. Traders use it to see where price sits relative to that average.

BOS (Break of Structure)

Price breaks an important swing level in the direction of the existing trend, potentially supporting continuation.

CHoCH (Change of Character)

Price breaks a meaningful structural level against the prior trend, suggesting the market's behavior may be changing.

Liquidity Zone

An area where orders may be concentrated, often around previous highs, lows, or obvious price levels.

Liquidity Sweep

Price briefly trades beyond an important high or low and then reverses back. This may indicate a failed breakout, but not every sweep produces a reversal.

Pullback

A temporary movement against the prevailing trend.

Stop-Loss / Take-Profit

Stop-Loss (SL) is an order intended to limit a losing trade; execution may differ from the stop price in fast markets. Take-Profit (TP) is a planned price level where a trader intends to close part or all of a profitable position.

How a Trading Setup Develops

A trading setup is more than a BUY or SELL label.

  1. Step 1: Identify direction.Is the market trending upward, trending downward, or moving sideways?
  2. Step 2: Find an important level.Look for previous highs, lows, VWAP, support, resistance, or liquidity zones.
  3. Step 3: Watch price behavior.Does price break a level, reject it, retest it, or continue through it?
  4. Step 4: Look for confirmation.A candle close, structural shift, or other supporting evidence can strengthen a setup. No confirmation guarantees success.
  5. Step 5: Plan the risk.Know your entry, stop-loss, potential target, and dollar risk before entering.
  6. Step 6: Manage the position.Monitor whether the original trade idea remains valid. A normal pullback doesn't always mean the trend has ended.

07

When NOT to Trade

Sometimes the strongest decision is waiting. Consider staying out when:

  • Price is moving sideways without a clear setup.
  • The stop-loss would require more risk than your plan allows.
  • Price has already traveled far beyond the intended entry.
  • A major economic announcement is approaching and volatility may increase.
  • The market is producing conflicting signals.
  • You're trying to recover losses emotionally.

Remember: you don't have to participate in every market move.
